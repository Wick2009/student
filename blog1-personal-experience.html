What Building for Real Users Actually Taught Me

Reflections on building software for the IDD community through the UESL platform.

May 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Posts Personal Experience Project Highlights Technical Deep Dive

When I started this project, I had a vague idea of what accessibility meant. I knew about ARIA labels, color contrast ratios, keyboard navigation. Those things felt like a checklist — do them, check them off, move on. What I did not expect was how quickly working on a platform for the IDD community would change that framing entirely.

The Unified Esports League serves individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Real people, at real tech centers across San Diego, using shared devices, navigating software that was not built with them in mind. When I sat down to build the Accessibility Toolkit and the UESL Coach chatbot, I kept coming back to that specificity. Not "users" in the abstract — specific users with specific needs that I needed to actually think through.

The chatbot is a good example. I could have pointed it at a general-purpose AI prompt and let it answer anything. Instead I wrote a system prompt that locks it to UESL-specific knowledge: the program's mission, how many tech centers there are, what ages they serve, who to contact for Spanish-language support. An IDD user who opens that chatbot and asks an off-topic question gets a gentle redirect rather than a confident wrong answer. That decision sounds small but it matters a lot when the person on the other end has less tolerance for confusion.

The same thinking shaped the Summarize Page feature. Instead of building a separate summary tool with a cold, clinical output, I routed it through the UESL Coach so the summary comes back in the same warm, encouraging voice. Someone using the site should not feel like they switched between two different products depending on what they need.

Pitching these features to Rajeev Pal, a VP at Qualcomm, felt different from any classroom presentation I have done. He was not evaluating whether I understood the concept of accessibility. He was looking at the actual product and asking real questions about it. That kind of feedback has a weight to it. When someone at that level engages seriously with something you built, it sharpens how you think about the decisions you made.

If I could go back, I would connect the standalone summarization endpoint I built to an actual interface earlier. I built /api/groq/analyze with support for summarization, sentiment analysis, and keyword extraction and then ran out of time to wire it to a frontend page. It works, but nobody can reach it yet. That gap between building something and making it usable is a lesson I will not forget.

Building for UESL reminded me that software is not finished when it compiles. It is finished when the person it was made for can use it without needing to ask for help.
