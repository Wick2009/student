The Accessibility Toolkit and UESL Coach: Building for the IDD Community

How the two features I built came together to serve individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities.

May 26, 2026 · 5 min read
Posts Personal Experience Project Highlights Technical Deep Dive

The Unified Esports League runs after-school gaming programs for individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities across seven tech centers in San Diego. The students are real, the program is real, and the platform we built needed to reflect that — not just look polished, but actually serve people who interact with technology in ways that most software ignores.

My contribution to the project was two connected features: a floating Accessibility Toolkit available on every page of the UESL site, and the UESL Coach, an AI assistant and page summarizer built on a custom Groq backend. They were designed to work together — the toolkit gives users control over how they experience the site, and the Coach gives them a way to understand what is on it.

The toolkit is a small button fixed in the bottom corner of every page. On click, it opens a panel with twelve controls organized around reading, vision, focus, and navigation. The Text-to-Speech reader speaks any highlighted text aloud using the browser's speech synthesis API — useful for users who process spoken language more easily than written text. The Dyslexia Font button loads OpenDyslexic, a typeface designed specifically for dyslexic readers, and applies it site-wide with increased letter and word spacing. High Contrast mode switches the entire site's color scheme to black, white, and yellow. Focus Mode fades out the navigation and footer so only the main content is visible. The Reading Ruler follows the cursor as a soft horizontal band across the screen, helping users track lines of text without losing their place. Font size controls let users increase or decrease text in two-pixel steps up to a comfortable ceiling.

None of these are novel ideas in isolation. What made the implementation meaningful is that they are available everywhere, not tucked into a settings page that most users would never find. Someone in the middle of reading an article can open the toolkit, switch to the dyslexia font, and keep reading without leaving the page. That frictionlessness was a deliberate decision.

The Chatbot and Summarize Page buttons connect to the UESL Coach. The Coach is not a general-purpose AI assistant — it is a domain-scoped chatbot that only knows about UESL. It knows the program's mission, its coaching ratios, its tech center locations, and its contact information. When a user asks it something unrelated, it redirects them gently instead of confidently answering something outside its scope. For an IDD audience, that predictability matters.

The Summarize Page button takes things a step further. It grabs the text from whatever page the user is currently on, sends it to the UESL Coach, and asks it to explain the content in simple terms. The response comes back in the same warm voice as the rest of the chatbot, not as a dry automated summary. A user who does not fully understand a page can get a plain-language version of it without leaving or asking anyone for help.

Every part of this was built around the idea that accessibility is not a feature you add at the end. It is a set of decisions you make throughout, and they only matter if the person who needs them can actually find and use them.
